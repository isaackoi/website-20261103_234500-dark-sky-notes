## The unit: magnitudes per square arcsecond

Sky brightness in this field is usually quoted in **magnitudes per square arcsecond** (mag/arcsec²): the apparent magnitude of a patch of sky one square arcsecond in size. The scale is inverted - larger numbers mean darker skies. Anchors worth memorising: a natural pristine sky measures about 21.9-22.0; a good rural dark site 21.0-21.8; a rural-suburban transition 20-21; a bright suburb 18-19; an urban centre 16-17.5. Each whole step of one magnitude is a factor of about 2.5 in brightness, so a city at 17.0 is roughly 100 times brighter than a pristine site at 22.0 - the difference between a handful of stars and the Milky Way casting shadows.

The subtlety that trips up newcomers: the human eye's naked-eye limiting magnitude is related to this number but not linearly, because the eye adapts and integrates differently. Two skies 0.5 mag/arcsec² apart can differ dramatically in what you can see, and humidity can shift a single site by more than that in one night. Hence the professional's rule: **one reading is anecdote; a monitored site is data.**

## Ground instruments

**Sky quality meters (SQM).** The handheld workhorse: a photodiode behind a fish-eye lens, reading zenith brightness in a few seconds, accurate to a few hundredths of a magnitude when used with care (dark-adapted, away from obstructions, multiple readings averaged). Their broadband detector does not record spectrum, which matters when comparing amber-lit and white-lit towns - two sites can read identically while looking nothing alike.

**All-sky cameras.** A camera with a fish-eye lens and photometric calibration produces a brightness map of the entire sky dome, from which researchers derive integrated indicators - the natural-sky-luminance-normalised zenith, the brightness-weighted average of the dome, the horizon-direction maximum. The methodology literature shows how such maps are calibrated and which indicators are robust to camera and site differences, which is what makes cross-site comparisons defensible.[1] A methods-and-challenges review covering the whole toolbox - meters, cameras, drones, and satellites - is the standard reference for what each instrument measures and its systematic errors.[2]

**DSLR cameras.** A consumer camera on a tripod with fixed settings, calibrated against star fields, gives repeatable sky brightness for the price of a camera many people already own - the basis of most academic citizen-science campaigns.

## Satellites: the view from orbit - and its blind spot

The satellite series behind most world maps (including the famous light-pollution globes) is sensitive in a broad visible-to-near-infrared band. It measures **upward radiance** - light leaving the ground - not sky brightness at any observer. From these radiance data, the atlas models compute artificial sky brightness at ground level; the modelling chain and its validation are documented in the world atlas papers.[3]

The blind spot matters and is increasingly cited: the satellite band under-weights the short-wavelength blue light that LED street lighting emits heavily - and that both scatters most in the atmosphere ([How skyglow works]({{ 'how-skyglow-works/' | relative_url }})) and matters most biologically ([Wildlife and artificial light]({{ 'wildlife-and-artificial-light/' | relative_url }}); Light, health and sleep). A city that swapped sodium for blue-rich LED can look *dimmer to the satellite* while its sky got brighter. Radiance-vs-extent analysis of the satellite record over the LED transition era shows global upward light still increasing - about 2% per year in radiance and extent in the early 2010s - with the true increase in visually and biologically effective light probably larger than the satellite record shows.[4]

## Citizen science: Globe at Night and the star-count trend

The Globe at Night programme asks participants to match the constellation they see (Orion, Leo, Cygnus, Crux, depending on season and hemisphere) against magnitude charts, recording the faintest star visible - a naked-eye measurement with defined error characteristics, repeated worldwide for over a decade. The 2023 analysis of nearly 45,000 such observations from 2011 to 2022 found that visible sky quality is degrading fast: expressed as sky brightness, the global aggregate corresponds to an increase of about 9.6% per year - far faster than ground-based satellite estimates implied - with the fastest degradation in North America and Europe, where most measurements come from and where most people live under already-bright skies.[5]

That number deserves its caveats: observations cluster where observers are (urban, wealthy, northern-hemisphere skies), naked-eye limiting magnitude is a coarse ordinal scale, and annual weather and volcanic aerosols move individual years. The study's authors addressed the major biases, and no plausible correction erases the signal: the star-filled sky is disappearing from human experience on a timescale of years, not centuries.

## How to measure your own sky, honestly

A defensible personal baseline takes about fifteen minutes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Pick a zenith spot with a clear horizon, note date, time, moon phase, and weather.
2. Let your eyes dark-adapt ten minutes; no phone in that window.
3. Find the seasonal campaign constellation and match it to a Globe at Night magnitude chart; record the limiting magnitude (naked-eye) - the programme's instructions walk you through it.[6]
4. Optionally average 3-5 SQM readings taken a minute apart.
5. Repeat monthly, same site, same conditions class - the value is in the series, not the number.

</div>

If you are choosing where to observe or holiday, the interactive maps built from the atlas data are a legitimate first filter[3][7] - cross-check them against recent local measurements, because maps age faster than skies in either direction.
