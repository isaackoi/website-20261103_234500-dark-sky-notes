## Introduction

This guide defines each term the way researchers and lighting professionals use it, explains how darkness is graded and measured in plain numbers, and clears up the most common confusion: the difference between the light you can see being wasted and the light the atmosphere returns to you as a glowing dome.

## The four problems

### Skyglow

Skyglow is the brightening of the night sky itself. Some of it is natural - airglow, scattered starlight, and zodiacal light exist even at the darkest sites - but the dominant form over cities is artificial: light from ground sources scattered by air molecules and airborne particles, returning to the ground as a diffuse glow. Skyglow is why the urban sky looks grey or orange instead of black, why only the brightest stars survive, and why the Milky Way is invisible to more than a third of the world's people.[1] Its physics - and the surprising importance of the colour of the lamp - is the subject of [How skyglow works]({{ 'how-skyglow-works/' | relative_url }}).

### Glare

Glare is light that enters the eye directly and reduces your ability to see. It comes in two flavours that matter outdoors: **disability glare**, which scatters inside the eye and washes out the scene (an unshielded floodlight above a driveway, seen head-on, can make the intruder behind it invisible), and **discomfort glare**, which is merely unpleasant but fatiguing. Glare is a safety problem masquerading as a safety feature: beyond a point, more brightness makes people feel safer while actually seeing less.

### Light trespass

Light trespass is light that crosses a property boundary - the neighbour's porch lamp painting a stripe across your bedroom ceiling, a car-park fixture lighting an apartment two floors up. It is the form of light pollution most likely to reach human sleep, because windows are where outdoor light becomes indoor light. Trespass is also the easiest to legislate, because a boundary makes the harm concrete.

### Clutter and overlighting

Clutter is the visual chaos of competing bright signs and fixtures; overlighting is simply more light than the task needs. Both matter because they are the upstream cause of the other three: unshielded, redundant, and excessive installations are what send light into eyes, windows, and the sky. The world night-sky-brightness atlas found that the artificially lit area of the planet keeps expanding and that radiance - the brightness of that lit area - rose in most countries studied, evidence that efficient LED lighting has so far been used to light more, not less.[2]

## How dark is "dark"? Grading the night

Because "you should see my sky" is not evidence, observers grade darkness in numbers. Three scales dominate.

**Naked-eye limiting magnitude.** The faintest star a dark-adapted eye can see straight up, expressed in astronomical magnitude (each magnitude is about 2.5 times fainter). Under a pristine sky, magnitude 6.5 or fainter; under bright urban skyglow, perhaps 3.5 or worse - a loss of tens of visible stars out of thousands.

**The Bortle scale.** A nine-class scale from 1 (pristine, astronomer's gold: the zodiacal band casts shadows, the Southern sky glows faintly) to 9 (inner-city: the sky is grey or orange, fewer than a dozen stars visible). Bortle classes are defined by observable phenomena - whether the Milky Way casts shadows, whether M31 is visible - which makes them robust but coarse. Most suburban dwellers live under class 7 to 9; most have never seen anything darker than class 5.

**Instrument magnitudes per square arcsecond.** Sky-quality meters and all-sky cameras report sky brightness in magnitudes per square arcsecond (mag/arcsec²). Rough anchors: 22.0 is an excellent natural dark site, 19.5-20.5 is a good rural sky, 18 is a bright suburb, and 16-17 is a city centre. These are the numbers used by the world atlas and by every serious monitoring programme, and the conversion between them and what your eye perceives is not linear - a sky "twice as bright" in physical units can look dramatically worse to a dark-adapted observer.[3]

## The map is not the sky

Two cautions keep this vocabulary honest.

First, satellite maps of light pollution - the familiar glowing-globe images - measure light emitted *upward* at the wavelengths the satellite sees, not sky brightness as experienced on the ground. Modern blue-rich white LEDs are under-represented in the most-used satellite band, which means some of the most disruptive lighting is literally dimmer on the map than in the sky.[4] The atlas models correct for much of this, but any single map is an estimate, not a measurement of your backyard.

Second, sky brightness varies with weather, season, and airborne particles - a humid night glows more than a dry one at the same emission. A single reading is anecdote; a series of readings, at the same site, over months, is data. That is exactly the logic of the citizen-science star counts described in [Measuring darkness]({{ 'measuring-darkness/' | relative_url }}), which aggregated nearly a decade of observations and found visible sky quality declining globally by close to ten percent per year in the places people actually measured.[5]

## What light pollution means not

Two honest caveats. Darkness advocacy is not a demand for dark streets: the goal of every serious dark-sky organisation is *well-directed* light, with fixtures that put light on the ground where people need it - a goal that aligns with safety, not against it.[6] And not every bright light is pollution: aviation, maritime, and some workplace lighting are legally or practically required. The question this site asks throughout is narrower and more useful - is the light doing its job, and is any of it escaping where it does harm? [Fixing light pollution]({{ 'fixing-light-pollution/' | relative_url }}) works through that door.
