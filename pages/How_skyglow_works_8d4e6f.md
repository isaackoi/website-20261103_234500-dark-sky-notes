## The two routes upward

Light reaches the sky by two routes with different consequences.

**Direct emission** is light that leaves the fixture already aimed upward: the unshielded globe lamp, the wall pack tilted 45 degrees, the advertising sign lit from below, the laser-like beam of a floodlight missed by its mounting bracket. Direct emission is pure waste - by definition it lights nothing on the ground - and it is the component a full-cutoff fixture eliminates completely. Fixing fixture geometry is the one intervention with no trade-offs at all.

**Reflected emission** is ground-reflected light: illumination that does its job on the pavement and then bounces. Roughly a tenth of ground light reflects upward from typical road and roof surfaces; pale surfaces reflect more. Reflected light is the irreducible floor of skyglow - you can only reduce it by lighting less - and it is why even perfectly shielded cities still glow.

The split matters because the two routes respond differently to policy. Shielding laws eliminate the first; dimming, curfews, and lower reflectance trim the second. A city that only shields still glows; a city that only dims still wastes direct uplight.

## What the atmosphere does with it

Once light is in the air, two scattering processes send it back down.

**Rayleigh scattering** - scattering off molecules of air themselves - is strongly wavelength-dependent, varying as one over the fourth power of wavelength. Blue light at 450 nanometres scatters roughly two and a half times more than red light at 700 nm. This is the same physics that makes the daytime sky blue, operating at night on city light.

**Mie scattering** - scattering off particles: haze, water droplets, dust - is much less colour-dependent and much stronger in absolute terms when the air is dirty or humid. Mie scattering is why a humid or hazy night over a city glows so much more than a dry, crisp one at identical emission: the particles act as a suspended screen.

The practical consequences follow directly. Skyglow is worse in humid climates and in haze. It is worse downwind of urban aerosols. And - the part that decides fixture policy - because the scattered share of blue light is intrinsically larger, *the colour of the lamp changes the skyglow even at identical lumens on the ground*. Field and modelling studies of light-source spectral power distribution find that blue-rich white sources (correlated colour temperature of about 4000 K and above) produce substantially more visible and environmentally effective skyglow than amber or low-pressure sodium equivalents delivering the same ground illumination.[1]

## Spectrum: the LED shift

For decades the classic urban glow was orange, because low-pressure sodium lamps emit almost all their light in a narrow yellow band. That spectrum was terrible for colour rendering but gentle on the sky: narrow-band yellow scatters relatively little and is far from the peak sensitivity of the dark-adapted human eye (and of many ecological light sensors).

The LED transition changed the spectrum. Early municipal LED retrofits, optimised for efficiency and colour rendering, used white light rich in short wavelengths around 450 nm - exactly the band that Rayleigh scattering loves, that the dark-adapted eye is most sensitive to, and that the human circadian system monitors. Modelling work comparing lamp spectra at matched ground brightness shows the skyglow penalty of blue-rich white clearly; amber and warm-white (2700 K or lower, and better still narrow-band amber) trade a little colour rendering for a large reduction in sky impact.[1] Meanwhile satellite radiance measurements over the LED transition era show total uplight radiance continuing to rise in most of the world - efficiency gains converted into more light rather than less - with the caveat that the satellite band most used for tracking under-detects blue-rich sources, so the true growth in blue light is larger than the record shows.[2]

## How far skyglow travels

Skyglow is a regional pollutant, not a local one. A large metro area visibly brightens the sky 50-100 kilometres away; the world atlas computes artificial brightness above natural thresholds across entire countries. Three mechanisms spread it:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Scattered light has no loyalty to its source.** A photon scattered downward over your dark observing site may have been emitted by a fixture 80 km away.
2. **The bright dome competes with the horizon.** Even when the zenith over a rural site stays dark, the city dome above the horizon destroys low-altitude observing - where comets, aurorae, and much deep-sky work live.
3. **Aerosols amplify distance effects.** Particle layers between you and a distant city scatter its light toward you efficiently, which is why distant domes bloom on hazy nights.

</div>

This geography is why dark-sky protection is organised at landscape scale - reserves and parks covering hundreds of square kilometres, described in [Where the dark remains]({{ 'where-the-dark-remains/' | relative_url }}) - and why a single well-lit distribution warehouse on the edge of a dark region is a regional, not a local, problem.

## The honest numbers on how bad it is

The atlas work gives the scale of the problem in three statements, each worth quoting carefully. More than 80% of the world's population lives under skies brighter than the natural baseline; for the United States and Europe the figure exceeds 99%. Roughly a third of humanity - including about 60% of Europeans and nearly 80% of North Americans - cannot see the Milky Way from where they live.[3] And citizen-science star counts between 2011 and 2022 found the naked-eye sky degrading fast enough to correspond to sky brightness growing on the order of ten percent per year, far faster than satellite-only estimates suggested.[4]

## From mechanism to fix

Every rule in the practical guides falls out of this physics: shield fixtures (kill direct emission), dim and trim hours (cut both routes), choose warm or amber spectrum (cut the scattering and the biological effectiveness of what escapes), and control reflections where you can. None of it requires darkness as a lifestyle; it requires light aimed, sized, timed, and coloured for its job - the checklist in Fixing light pollution.
