## Why night matters biologically

Three facts about nocturnal biology explain most of the damage.

**Circadian and circannual clocks are light-set.** Nearly every organism uses light, especially the blue end of twilight, as the primary time signal for daily and seasonal rhythms. Night lighting delivers a fake dawn cue at the wrong hour.

**Darkness is a resource.** For prey species, night is cover; for nocturnal predators, it is a workspace. Light changes the terms of a predator-prey arms race instantly - favouring, depending on the system, either the predator that hunts by the new light or the prey that avoids it.

**Natural light cues are faint and directional.** Sea turtles find the sea by moving toward the bright, low horizon over the water; moths navigate by maintaining a constant angle to the distant moon; migratory birds calibrate by star patterns and by the horizon's polarised twilight. These systems work because natural cues are dim and far away. A porch lamp, a streetlight, or a lit tower is bright and near - it outshouts the cue the animal's navigation was built around.

## Case study: sea-turtle hatchlings

On a natural beach, hatchling loggerhead and green turtles emerge from the nest at night and crawl toward the brighter horizon over the ocean, where starlight and moonlight reflect off the surf. Experimental work as early as 2007 demonstrated the mechanism cleanly: hatchlings exposed to artificial lighting orient toward it and away from the sea, and the disorientation persists as long as the light dominates their field of view.[2] On developed coastlines the result is lethal and well documented: hatchlings crawl inland toward dune lighting, roadways, and pool decks, dying of exhaustion, dehydration, predation, or traffic. A 2023 review of coastal development impacts on sea-turtle conservation identifies unshielded beachfront lighting as a leading, and fixable, driver of hatchling mortality, with lighting ordinances and amber narrow-spectrum fixtures as the demonstrated remedies.[3]

The case is instructive because the fix works: Florida-style lighting codes (shield fixtures, low-mount, long-wavelength lamps, curfews during hatchling season) measurably reduce disorientation. Wildlife lighting is one of the few areas where the engineering guidance is settled.

## Case study: night-migrating birds

Hundreds of millions of birds migrate at night, navigating partly by star patterns and magnetism, and drawn - for reasons still under study - to bright artificial lights. The definitive experimental demonstration comes from New York City's Tribute in Light memorial: two vertical columns of intense light over lower Manhattan. Radar and acoustic monitoring around the installation found that when the beams were on, birds aggregated in high densities within the lit volume, circled, called frequently, and slowed their migration; when operators shut the beams off for even short periods, the birds resumed normal migration within minutes. The installation now runs with automated shut-offs triggered by radar detection of high bird densities - a rare example of a harm being switched off literally on demand.[4]

The same attraction kills at scale: tall lit structures and bright low buildings cause fatal collisions, which is why "lights out" campaigns during spring and autumn migration - voluntary building-darkening programmes in dozens of North American and European cities - are among the most cost-effective conservation actions available, costing building operators almost nothing.

## Case study: insects

Insects are in broad decline, and light is one of the named drivers. A widely cited 2020 review gathered experimental and observational evidence across taxa - moths drawn to lamps and killed or exhausted, mayflies whose mating swarms are disrupted by bridges of light, dung beetles that lose their celestial orientation compass - and argued that light pollution acts on insects through attraction, repulsion, and the re-engineering of entire nocturnal communities, interacting with the better-known drivers of habitat loss and pesticides.[5] The review's practical warning is that even rural, low-density lighting can matter: a single farmyard lamp restructures the local insect community in a radius of metres to tens of metres, and the effect compounds along every road and yard.

## Plants and the wider community

Light also reaches organisms that cannot move away. An experimental and synthesis programme on wild plants found artificial night light altering flowering phenology, bud burst, leaf retention, and herbivore interactions - effects that propagate to the insects and birds that depend on those timing cues.[6] The mechanistic review literature emphasises that these are community-level reorganisations, not just individual stress: light changes when plants flower, when pollinators visit, when predators hunt, and how species assemble.[1]

## What the evidence supports in practice

Four rules carry the strongest evidence for wildlife-sensitive lighting:

1. **Spectrum first.** Long-wavelength (amber, ~589 nm or longer) light at matched illuminance attracts and disrupts markedly less than broad white or blue-rich light - across turtles, insects, and many birds. This aligns exactly with the skyglow physics in [How skyglow works]({{ 'how-skyglow-works/' | relative_url }}).

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

2. **Shield and lower.** A fixture that emits nothing above horizontal and is mounted low puts light on the task and removes the bright cue from the horizon and the sky.
3. **Time-limit.** Motion sensors, part-night dimming, and seasonal curfews (hatchling season, migration nights) remove the harm during exactly the hours it matters.
4. **Kill redundant light.** The cheapest fix is deletion - many wildlife-conflicting installations serve no task at all after midnight.
