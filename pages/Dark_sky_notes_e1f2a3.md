It is written for three readers at once: the backyard astronomer who wants to understand why the horizon glows orange, the homeowner or facilities manager deciding which fixtures to buy, and the council officer or community volunteer who wants a lighting policy that keeps streets safe without washing out the sky.

## What is actually at stake

The costs of wasted light fall into three buckets, each covered by a guide in this series.

**The sky itself.** Skyglow is the cumulative glow produced by light scattered off molecules and aerosols. It is strongest near cities but travels far: observers tens of kilometres from a large metro area still see their zenith brightened by it. The physics of that scattering - and the reason the colour of a lamp matters as much as its wattage - is covered in [How skyglow works]({{ 'how-skyglow-works/' | relative_url }}).

**Living things.** Night is a habitat. Artificial light alters reproduction, foraging, migration, and orientation across an enormous range of species - from sea-turtle hatchlings that circle inland toward lit resorts instead of toward the moonlit sea, to the hundreds of millions of birds that migrate at night and are drawn off course by tall lit structures. The evidence base, including experimental work that turned city lights off and watched bird behaviour change within minutes, is reviewed in [Wildlife and artificial light]({{ 'wildlife-and-artificial-light/' | relative_url }}).

**Human sleep and circadian health.** The human circadian system is exquisitely sensitive to short-wavelength light in the evening. Controlled laboratory studies show that reading on a self-lit screen before bed delays melatonin release and shifts the body clock relative to reading a printed book under dim light.[3] Outdoor artificial light at night has been associated - with the usual caveats of observational research - with disrupted sleep and with elevated risks of hormone-related cancers in heavily exposed urban populations.[4][5] The strength and limits of that evidence are laid out in Light, health and sleep.

## The reason for optimism

Light pollution is unusually fixable, for a pollutant. It produces no legacy residue: turn a fixture off or shield it properly and the harm stops immediately, with no cleanup. The fixes are mostly known and mostly cheap:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

- **Shielding.** A full-cutoff fixture directs light where it is needed and none into the sky. Fixing fixture geometry alone can eliminate most direct upward emission.
- **Right-sizing.** Many outdoor installations are simply brighter than their task requires. Dimming, trimming hours with timers and motion sensors, and deleting redundant fixtures cut both light and electricity bills.
- **Warmer spectrum.** Blue-rich white light (4000 K and above) scatters more in the atmosphere and suppresses melatonin more effectively than amber or warm-white sources of equal lumen output.[6] Choosing 2700 K or lower, or narrow-spectrum amber, reduces both impacts.

</div>

The practical side - from a single porch lamp to a municipal ordinance - is worked through in [Fixing light pollution]({{ 'fixing-light-pollution/' | relative_url }}), and the places that have gone furthest, certified dark-sky reserves and parks where the Milky Way still casts a shadow, are described in [Where the dark remains]({{ 'where-the-dark-remains/' | relative_url }}).

## How to use these guides

Each guide is self-contained and can be read in any order. If you want one starting point:

- New to the subject? Begin with [What light pollution means]({{ 'what-light-pollution-means/' | relative_url }}), which defines skyglow, glare, trespass, and clutter, and explains how darkness is measured and graded.
- Planning work on your own property? Go straight to Fixing light pollution and its fixture checklist.
- Trying to make a case to a council or community association? How skyglow works and [Measuring darkness]({{ 'measuring-darkness/' | relative_url }}) give you the numbers; Fixing light pollution gives you the policy vocabulary.
- Teaching or writing about the subject? Measuring darkness shows how professional and citizen-science measurements are actually made, including the global star-count programme whose 2011-2022 results showed visible sky quality degrading by close to a tenth per year.[7]

Every claim in these guides is sourced in numbered endnotes to the underlying study or institutional reference, with a Further reading list of verified starting points and short video explainers embedded where they genuinely help.

## A note on honesty and uncertainty

Evidence in this field is uneven, and these guides do not flatten that unevenness. Experimental studies - lights turned on and off in a controlled way - exist for some ecological questions and almost no human-health questions. Human outdoor-lighting epidemiology is observational, exposure is usually estimated from satellite data that under-detects blue-rich LED light, and confounding by urbanisation is real. Where the literature is mixed, the guides say so. Light at night is not a poison with a linear dose-response curve, and darkness advocacy is not a demand to abolish night lighting; streets, workplaces, and homes need to be lit, and the entire purpose of this site is to light them well.
