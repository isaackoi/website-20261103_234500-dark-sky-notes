## The mechanism: a light-sensitive clock in your eye

Human circadian timing runs on a master clock in the hypothalamus, entrained each day by light detected not by the rods and cones that build images, but by a separate population of retinal ganglion cells containing the photopigment melanopsin. These cells are most sensitive to short-wavelength (blue, roughly 480 nm) light, they respond to the bright end of indoor lighting, and they feed the pineal gland, whose hormone melatonin rises in darkness and signals biological night to every tissue in the body.

Two consequences follow. **Colour matters**: a warm 2700 K lamp suppresses melatonin less than a cool white one of equal brightness, and dim amber hardly at all. **Timing matters**: the same light that is harmless at noon is a potent clock-shifter in the two to three hours before habitual bedtime. Outdoor light trespass through a window is typically far dimmer than a reading lamp - but it arrives all night, including the hours when even small doses keep sleep lighter.

## The strongest evidence: controlled experiments

The cleanest demonstration comes from a randomised crossover study in which participants read before bed for five nights, once on a light-emitting e-reader and once with a printed book under dim light. The e-reader condition suppressed melatonin by about 55%, delayed melatonin onset by more than an hour, delayed the timing of the body clock, reduced next-morning alertness, and shortened subjective sleep quality - measured, not asked.[1] This is not a study of streetlights, but it is the proof of mechanism: ordinary evening light, at ordinary indoor intensities, measurably retunes human circadian biology. Everything claimed about outdoor light and sleep leans on this mechanism.

## The middle tier: outdoor light and population health

A 2023 literature review of outdoor artificial light at night and human health surveys the observational record: outdoor ALAN exposure (usually estimated from satellite data) is associated with delayed sleep timing, shortened sleep, worse self-reported rest, and - in the cancer literature - elevated risks of hormone-related cancers, with the strongest signals for breast cancer.[2] The single most-cited study in that literature used Spanish nighttime satellite data and cancer registry data to estimate outdoor ALAN exposure for individuals: blue-enriched outdoor light exposure was associated with breast and prostate cancer risk in dose-response fashion.[3]

The caveats deserve respect, because they are the difference between evidence and alarmism:

- **Exposure misclassification.** Satellite estimates of *outdoor* light are a crude proxy for the light reaching a sleeping person's retina - through curtains, from a window facing an alley or a park. Blue-rich LED is under-detected by the satellite band most used, biasing the exposure variable.[3]
- **Confounding by urbanisation.** Bright neighbourhoods differ from dark ones in noise, shift work, air pollution, income, and health behaviours. The better studies adjust for these; no adjustment is perfect.
- **Ecological scope.** Associations are at the level of populations and addresses, not measured bedroom photometry.

The defensible summary: there is a plausible mechanism proved in the lab, and consistent but observational population associations that point the same direction. That is a strong basis for precaution in lighting policy - and an honest basis for personal action - without claiming that outdoor light alone is a proven cause of disease in an individual.

## The weakest tier: claims to treat with care

Some widely shared claims do not survive scrutiny. "Blue light from screens damages your retina" has no good epidemiological support at ordinary exposure levels - the melatonin and clock effects are real; the retinal-damage claims are extrapolations from extreme laboratory doses. "LED streetlights are toxic" conflates the light (a real circadian concern, dose- and spectrum-dependent) with electronics and radio-control claims that have no evidential basis. The guide's working rule: any claim about light and health should name the dose (illuminance at the eye), the spectrum, and the timing - claims that skip all three are usually folklore.

## What actually helps at night

The interventions supported by the mechanism and the trials literature, in rough order of effect per effort:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Darken the last two hours.** Dim household light and shift to warm sources after dinner; the e-reader study shows the payoff of moving in the opposite direction.[1]
2. **Fix trespass before fixtures.** Blackout curtains, door-draught seals on light corridors, and low-glare porch fixtures pointed away from windows remove the all-night exposure that you cannot otherwise control.
3. **Screen time as light, not sin.** The issue with late screens is brightness and colour, not the screen per se: night-shift modes and dimming help exactly insofar as they cut short-wavelength output and retinal illuminance.
4. **Morning light closes the loop.** Bright light in the first hour after waking anchors the clock and makes the evening discipline easier to hold.

</div>
