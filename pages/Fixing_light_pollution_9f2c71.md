## The five principles

DarkSky International distils responsible outdoor lighting into five principles, each of which maps directly onto the physics and biology covered elsewhere in this series:[1]

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Useful.** Light should serve a clear purpose. Start by asking whether a fixture is needed at all at that location and hour - deletion is the cheapest fix and the one no manufacturer will suggest.
2. **Targeted.** Light should go where it is needed and nowhere else. Full-cutoff fixtures emit nothing above the horizontal plane; aimed fixtures put the beam on the path, not the neighbours' windows or the sky.
3. **Low level.** Use the minimum intensity for the task. Most outdoor installations exceed recommended illuminance by a wide margin; glare and adaptation make over-lit spaces feel *less* safe, not more.
4. **Controlled.** Light should be on only when needed: motion sensors for low-traffic areas, timers, part-night schedules, and curfews for decorative and commercial lighting.
5. **Warm-coloured.** Choose the warmest colour that still meets the visual task - 2700 K or lower for general use, amber or narrow-band for wildlife-sensitive sites - because short-wavelength light drives skyglow and circadian disruption hardest.[2][3]

</div>

A fixture that satisfies all five is "dark-sky friendly" in the meaningful sense - not a marketing label, but a checkable description: shielded, right-sized, timed, warm.

## The homeowner's checklist

Work through your property in this order; each step takes minutes and compounds with the rest.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

- **Audit at night.** Walk your property after dark from the pavement and from the neighbours' side. Look for: fixtures visible as bare light sources from off the property (glare and trespass), light striking windows (yours and others'), and any fixture shining upward (pure waste).
- **Shield or replace floodlights.** A full-cutoff wall pack or shielded porch fixture that lights the door and step - not the street, not the sky - typically cuts light leaving the property by more than half while improving what you can actually see.
- **Aim and mount.** Floodlights belong horizontal-or-below, mounted at eaves height or lower; every degree of upward tilt buys skyglow and loses task light.
- **Downgrade wattage.** Outdoor LEDs are cheap and bright; most residential needs are met by fixtures in the 300-800 lumen range. If you can read a book comfortably under it, it is brighter than a path needs.
- **Add controls.** A motion sensor converts an all-night 800-lumen fixture into a 3-minute-on-demand light - less energy, less emission, and arguably better security (sudden light draws attention; constant light is wallpaper).
- **Warm it.** When replacing, choose 2700 K or 2200 K. At equal lumens on the ground, the warm choice scatters less in the atmosphere and suppresses melatonin less.[2]

</div>

## The vocabulary of policy

Councils, planners, and community groups arguing for better lighting need these terms in writing:

**Full cutoff / ULR.** A fixture whose uplight ratio (ULR) is zero - no light above horizontal. Procurement language should specify ULR ≤ 0% (or the closest standard class) rather than "dark-sky friendly".

**Correlated colour temperature (CCT) caps.** Policy language increasingly caps street lighting at 3000 K, with 2700 K common; wildlife-sensitive zones specify amber (PC-amber, ~1800-2200 K, or 589 nm low-pressure sodium equivalents). The justification is in the skyglow and biology literature, not aesthetics.[2][3]

**Lighting zones (LZ0-LZ4).** A standard framework from darkest natural areas (LZ0 - no ambient lighting, national-park cores) to high-activity urban cores (LZ4), each zone with caps on lumens, ULR, and curfew requirements. Zones let a single ordinance treat a city centre and a rural edge differently without ad hoc negotiation.

**Curfew hours.** A time after which non-essential lighting must dim or extinguish (commonly 22:00 or midnight). Curfews are the cheapest large-scale intervention available to a municipality.

**Illuminance, not lumens, for tasks.** Specify the measured light level needed on the surface (lux on the path, at the door), and let bidders meet it with the least emitted light - this is how procurement avoids a brightness arms race.

## The LED retrofit trap

The great practical lesson of the last decade: efficiency is not reduction. When cities replaced sodium streetlights with efficient white LEDs, the satellite record showed upward radiance and the total lit area continuing to grow - the energy savings were converted into more and brighter light rather than into dark.[4] Three specific traps recur:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

- **Over-lighting "because we can."** LED efficacy makes it cheap to exceed every illuminance recommendation; specifications must cap lux, not just watts.
- **Blue-rich white by default.** Early LED stock favoured 4000 K+ for efficacy; 2700 K and PC-amber options now exist at small efficiency penalties and large sky-and-biology gains.[2]
- **Worse glare geometry.** High-output LEDs in poorly shielded housings concentrate luminance into harsh points - pedestrian-level glare complaints rose after several retrofits that ignored fixture optics.

</div>

A retrofit specified with lux caps, 2700 K-or-lower CCT, full-cutoff fixtures, and curfew dimming delivers the energy win without the sky loss. One that only swaps the source makes the problem worse while feeling modern.

## Making the case where you live

Practical sequencing for local change: start with the inventory (what the council lights, when, at what CCT), because you cannot argue with unmeasured light; ask for trial areas (one street at 2700 K with dimming is a cheap pilot that sceptics can visit); align with money (the controls that cut light cut maintenance and energy budgets); and bring the wildlife hours - migration windows and hatchling seasons are when curfews buy the most biology per concession.
